# Czech Rap

## Genre Overview

Czech rap is hip-hop performed in the Czech language. Its scene grew up in Prague and the regional cities after 1989, and it shares one market with Slovak rap. The recording usually cited as the first Czech hip-hop track is "Jižák" (1985) by Lesík Hajdovský's band Manželé. Hajdovský wrote it after hearing Grandmaster Flash on West German radio, and he swapped New York for Prague's Jižní Město housing estate. Before the 1989 revolution, rap was a curiosity used to liven up rock and funk sets. A real scene formed in the early 1990s. WWW ("We Want Words") started in Prague in the early decade and later moved toward experimental electro-industrial rap. Peneři strýčka Homeboye (PSH) formed in 1993 around Orion and DJ Shitness; Vladimir 518 joined in the late 1990s and DJ Mike Trafik in 2003. Chaozz formed in Prague in 1995 and broke through with "Policijééé" from their 1996 debut album, and Indy & Wich (Indy and DJ Wich, joined by LA4 from about 1999) followed in 1998. PSH's *Repertoár* (2001), which included the hometown anthem "Praha", became the first big non-commercial Czech hip-hop success. Indy & Wich's single "Hlasuju proti" (2001) and album *My 3* (2002) set the sample-based, DJ-driven boom bap template.

The 2000s widened the scene. Orion went solo with *Teritorium* (2003) and *Teritorium II* (2005), which won the Anděl award for best hip-hop & R&B album. Prago Union formed around 2002, led by ex-Chaozz rapper Kato with DJ Skupla. They released *HDP* (2005) and later won the Anděl hip-hop album award for *Dezorient Express* (2010). Supercrooo (Hugo Toxxx and James Cole, 2004–2007) released *Toxic Funk* (2004) and *České kuře: Neurofolk* (2005). From 2005 a gangsta-rap wave led by Hugo Toxxx turned diss tracks into public events: his "Numero Uno" (2005) went after Marpo. Vladimir 518's Bigg Boss label, formalised in the mid-2000s, became the base of the hardcore circle. Other labels were Ty Nikdy (Paulie Garand, Rest), Azurit Kingdom (Pastor, Sergei Barracuda) and Ektor's Detektor Records. Hip Hop Kemp in Hradec Králové (2002–2019) gave the scene an annual festival. Czech and Slovak are mutually intelligible, and the two countries shared a state until 1993, so Slovak acts were part of the same market from the start. Kontrafakt (Rytmus, Ego and DJ Anys, founded 2001) debuted with *E.R.A.* (2004). Rytmus's solo *Král* (2009) produced "Zlatokopky", Majk Spirit released *Nový človek* (2011), and Separ ran from *Buldozér* (2012) to *Flowdemort* (2023). The early 2010s added more variety. Smack One, described as the first Czech grime rapper, released *2051* (2012). Horrorcore rapper Řezník was tried and acquitted in 2011 over the song "Konečné řešení". Marpo released *R!OT* (2013) and later moved from rap-rock to country rap.

The turn toward trap began around 2012, when underground acts such as Tyler Durden and his group Gynger Money Gang brought a new wave of rappers onto the scene. The breakthrough came with Yzomandias (Jakub Vlček, born 1991), usually called the pioneer of Czech trap. He recorded as Logic before co-founding the YZO Empire collective (2012) and the Milion+ label (2015). The Milion+ roster, including Nik Tendo, Robin Zoot, Hasan, Jickson and Karlo, defined the streaming-era sound: 808-heavy beats, money and brand flexing ("Kawasaki", 2016) and frequent features between label mates. Melodic, Auto-Tuned rap became the commercial center. Viktor Sheen (born 1993 in Almaty) left Blakkwood Records and released a run of albums from *Jungler* (2017) to *Impostor syndrom* (2024), which won an Anděl. Calin (born 1997 in Chișinău) reached No. 1 with "Růže" (2019) and with "Safír" (2023), recorded with Viktor Sheen. Ben Cristovao and Sofian Medjmedj pulled rap toward pop and R&B. Drill arrived through two routes. One was 58G from Jihlava, whose *58 tape vol.1*, a mix of grime, trap and drill, won the Vinyla Discovery of the Year award for 2020. The other was UK-based Czech Roma rappers such as Dollar Prync and Nerokid, who rap in Czech and Romani over UK drill and grime beats. In Ostrava, Sergei Barracuda and Hard Rico keep crime and hustling stories at the center. Klára Trsková of the Czech Language Institute describes rap as a place where Czech is borrowing heavily from English (skrrt, dissovat, flow, track). She notes that rappers often reach for one-syllable English swear words instead of longer Czech ones that fit the rhythm less well.

## Characteristics

- **Instrumentation**: Boom bap era (1990s–2000s): sampled soul, funk and jazz loops, chopped breaks, a hard kick and snare, and turntable scratching from crew DJs (DJ Wich, DJ Mike Trafik); trap era (2015+): 808 sub-bass with long tails and glides, rolling and triplet hi-hats, claps on the half-time backbeat, and dark minor-key piano, bell and pad melodies, with guitar or piano loops on melodic tracks; drill: sliding 808s, syncopated hats and sparse minor-key melodies; modern production is almost entirely programmed
- **Vocals**: Czech-language rap, mostly by male rappers, delivered dry and close to the mic with clear consonants — clusters such as str-, čtvr- and zdr- and the ř sound need crisp articulation to stay intelligible; boom bap delivery is conversational and punchline-driven, hardcore is low and confrontational, trap uses Auto-Tuned, half-sung hooks with stacked ad-libs, and drill is deadpan and staccato; English slang is mixed in with Czech endings, and Slovak verses appear on cross-border features
- **Production**: Boom bap follows a DJ-producer model with warm, gritty samples and dry, vocal-forward mixes; trap has an 808-dominated low end, wide reverb-heavy pads, vocal tuning used as an effect, and polished, streaming-ready masters; drill uses sparse arrangements built around sliding 808s; melodic trap is lusher, with guitar or piano loops and softer 808s; across all styles the vocal sits on top of the mix so the Czech wordplay stays audible
- **Energy/Mood**: Pride in a hometown or district (Prague, Jihlava, the Sudety borderlands of Robin Zoot's Ústí nad Labem), sardonic humor, hedonism, money and brand flexing, hustling and street economics, confrontational diss tracks, horror and political provocation (Řezník), and late-night melancholy, love and self-doubt in melodic trap
- **Structure**: Boom bap uses 16-bar verses (one written line per bar, so 16 lines rhymed in couplets) with a 4–8 bar hook, often scratched; trap runs verse-hook-verse with 8–16 bar verses, a repeated hook and post-hook ad-libs, at a streaming length of about 2:30–3:30; features are standard, including Czech–Slovak collaborations
- **Tempo**: Boom bap 85–95 BPM; trap programmed at 130–150 BPM and felt in half-time at 65–75 BPM (Yzomandias's "Kawasaki" is listed at 145 BPM, Nik Tendo's "Není limit" at 150); melodic pop-trap 80–90 BPM or the 160 BPM double-time equivalent (Calin's "Růže" is listed at 80, Viktor Sheen's "Barvy" at 90); drill 140–145 BPM in half-time; Slovak trap runs up to about 150–160 BPM (Separ's "Víťaz" is listed at 148)

## Lyric Conventions

- **Default rhyme scheme**: AABB couplets, with the end rhyme landing on the last snare of the line. Trap hooks are often AAAA or a single repeated line. Internal rhyme chains run across a bar on top of the couplets ("Zase na trase, prachy v kapse, vztek v hlase").
- **Rhyme quality**: The Czech default is the two-syllable (feminine) rhyme: match from the vowel of the next-to-last syllable to the end, with matching vowel length on the held vowel (kůže – může, chleba – třeba, nechci slávu – nejsi v právu). A one-syllable rhyme counts only when the line ends in a one-syllable word (noc – moc, svět – zpět). The modern style relies on multisyllabic rhymes and assonance chains that match the vowel pattern ("dneska v noci" / "ze sna do tmy" both run e-a-o-i). Rhyme by sound, not spelling: i/y and í/ý sound the same, ů = ú, and final consonants devoice (keš – lež – běž).
- **Verse structure**: Each written line is one felt bar. In boom bap that is one bar, so a full 16-bar verse is 16 lines rhymed in couplets; in half-time trap and drill a felt bar is two programmed bars, so 8 lines already fill 16 programmed bars. For Suno, write the 8-line default either way (see Density/pacing): split a 16-line boom bap verse into two verses or cut it. Hooks run 4 lines. Budget syllables rather than words: 10–14 syllables per line in a 16th-note flow (16 slots per felt bar, so 16 at most), with paired lines within ±2 syllables of each other; the Bar Density by Tempo table in the [Czech Lyric Craft Reference](../../skills/lyric-writer/czech-craft.md) gives the band for each delivery. Syllabic r and l count as syllables (smrt = 1, Brno = 2, Vltava = 3). The diphthongs ou, au and eu count as one syllable each (kou-kat, pou-ze, au-to, eu-ro), except where a prefix meets the stem: there the two letters are separate vowels and separate syllables (ne-u-mím = 3, po-u-žít = 3, na-u-ka = 3, ne-ur-či-tý = 4).
- **Key rule**: Czech stress always falls on the first syllable of a word, and a one-syllable preposition takes the stress of the word after it ("do domu" is stressed DO-do-mu). Put word-initial syllables on the kick and the snare, and avoid landing a short non-initial syllable on the snare (u-LI-ce and ka-MA-rád sound foreign). Czech trap sometimes shifts stress on purpose as a flow device; for Suno, keep first-syllable placement as the default, shift stress only deliberately, and test the result. Vowel length (á, é, í/ý, ó, ú/ů) is duration, not stress, so long vowels can be held across a beat. Unstressed clitics (se, si, mi, ti, jsem, by) sit in second position in the clause and never carry the end rhyme. Full rules: [Czech Lyric Craft Reference](../../skills/lyric-writer/czech-craft.md).
- **Avoid**: Grammatical rhymes, where the two-syllable match comes only from a shared inflectional or derivational ending (dělala – volala, chodili – mluvili, volání – přání, s klukama – s holkama, zelenej – červenej) — Czech morphology makes them effortless, so they sound cheap. Pairs like dělat – volat or velkej – malej match only the final syllable, so by the rule above they are not rhymes at all, just shared endings. Also avoid stock pairs such as nebe – tebe, pairs that differ only by a prefix (hrát – prohrát), homophones used as rhymes (být – bít) unless the pun is deliberate, word order inverted to force a rhyme ("srdce mé" instead of "moje srdce"), filler words padding the bar (prostě, jako, fakt, vlastně), and word-for-word translations of English rap clichés.
- **Language mixing**: Colloquial Czech (obecná čeština) is the genre norm in Bohemia (dobrej, bejt, sem/seš/sou, dělaj, s klukama). It is not universal: Moravian and Ostrava rappers don't use the Bohemian -ej forms (dobrej, bejt) and draw on regional speech instead, such as short vowels and words like šak and ogar in Ostrava, or Brno hantec such as šalina and hokna, so match the artist's region. Keep one register for the whole song rather than switching between jsem and sem at random. English loanwords take Czech endings (flexit, dissovat, na beatu) and rhyme by their Czech pronunciation (beat [bít] – žít). Spell slang the Czech way (keš, lóve) and write numbers as words so Suno is less likely to switch to English pronunciation — test first; see [Czech Pronunciation](../../reference/suno/pronunciation-guide.md#czech-pronunciation). Use Slovak only for an intended Slovak feature verse.
- **Density/pacing (Suno)**: Default **8 lines/verse** at 130-150 BPM (felt half-time, 65-75 effective) or 85-95 BPM boom bap. Faster CZ/SK trap at 150-160 BPM (75-80 effective) keeps the same 8-line default. Czech words carry more syllables than English ones, so budget 10-14 syllables per line instead of counting words; for melodic delivery at ≤75 effective BPM, use at most 6 lines. Never exceed 8 lines. Topics: 1-2/verse.

## Subgenres & Styles

| Style | Description | Reference Artists |
|-------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Old School / Boom Bap** | The 1990s–2000s foundation: sampled soul, funk and jazz loops, a hard kick and snare at 85–95 BPM, and a crew DJ cutting scratched hooks. Verses are dense and punchline-driven, and often rooted in Prague street life. See [Boom Bap](../boom-bap/README.md) for the production template. | PSH, Chaozz, Indy & Wich, Orion |
| **Lyrical / Introspective Rap** | Sample-based beats in the boom bap lineage, with verses that put storytelling, introspection and technical wordplay ahead of street bravado. The Ty Nikdy label and producer DJ Wich carry this line from the old school into the 2020s. | Prago Union, Paulie Garand, Rest |
| **Gangsta / Hardcore Rap** | The mid-2000s wave of aggressive, provocative street rap: darker beats, low and confrontational delivery, drug and crime themes, and diss tracks fought in public. Horrorcore pushed the provocation into horror and political shock. See [Gangsta Rap](../gangsta-rap/README.md) and [Horrorcore](../horrorcore/README.md). | Hugo Toxxx, Supercrooo, Vladimir 518, Řezník |
| **Street & Crime Rap** | Rap about hustling, street economics and crime, centered on Ostrava and usually set over dark minor-key beats with heavy 808s. Narrative detail (money, deals, police) matters more than wordplay. Sergei Barracuda's *Pouliční ekonomická* series is the reference point. | Sergei Barracuda, Hard Rico, Pastor |
| **Czech Trap** | The Milion+-era mainstream sound from 2015 on: beats programmed at 130–150 BPM and felt in half-time, booming 808s, rolling hi-hats, and dark bell or piano melodies. | **Melodic & Cloud Trap** | Auto-Tuned, half-sung rap over lush, reverb-heavy pads, guitar or piano loops and softer 808s, often at 80–90 BPM or the double-time equivalent. Themes turn to love, melancholy and self-doubt, and the cloud end uses hazier, more atmospheric beats. See [Cloud Rap](../cloud-rap/README.md). | Viktor Sheen, Calin, Hasan |
| **Pop Rap & Crossover** | Rap built for radio and streaming playlists, with sung choruses, bright production, and pop or R&B melodies. Hooks that switch between Czech and English are common. See [Pop Rap](../pop-rap/README.md). | Ben Cristovao, Calin, Sofian Medjmedj |
| **Drill & Grime** | UK-derived styles at 140–145 BPM: sliding 808s, syncopated hi-hats, sparse minor-key melodies, and staccato, deadpan delivery. Smack One brought grime into Czech in the 2010s, and 58G from Jihlava mixed grime, trap and drill. UK-based Czech Roma rappers rap in Czech and Romani over UK drill beats. | **Slovak Rap (CZ/SK Scene)** | Slovak-language rap that shares one market with Czech rap. The two languages are mutually intelligible, and cross-border features are routine. Styles range from Kontrafakt's 2000s street rap to Separ's fast trap at roughly 148–156 BPM. | Rytmus, Kontrafakt, Majk Spirit, Separ |

## Reference Tracks

- **Manželé - "Jižák"** — The 1985 track usually cited as the first Czech hip-hop recording. Lesík Hajdovský wrote it after hearing Grandmaster Flash on West German radio, and he swapped New York for Prague's Jižní Město housing estate. It started the scene's oldest habit: rapping about one specific neighborhood.
- **Chaozz - "Policijééé"** — The breakthrough hit from the Prague crew's 1996 debut album *...a nastal chaos*, from the first wave of post-1989 Czech rap groups. The title stretches its final vowel, an early example of a Czech hook built on a held long vowel.
- **PSH - "Praha"** — From *Repertoár* (2001), and also released as a single in 2002. A sample-based boom bap anthem to the group's home city on the album that became the first big non-commercial Czech hip-hop success. It set the template for district-pride rap that runs through the scene's later work.
- **Indy & Wich - "Hlasuju proti"** — The 2001 single (released with "Cesta štrýtu") that came before *My 3* (2002). DJ Wich's sample-based production and the rapper-plus-DJ format made it a model for Czech boom bap, and it remains a reference point for that partnership.
- **Orion - "Teritorium"** — Title track of Orion's 2003 solo debut. It carried the PSH member into a solo career. The *Teritorium* series continued with an Anděl-winning sequel (2005) and *Teritorium III* (2021).
- **Kontrafakt - "E.R.A."** — From the Slovak trio's 2004 debut *E.R.A.* Rytmus, Ego and DJ Anys made Slovak-language street rap a fixture of the shared Czech-Slovak market, where collaborations across the language line have been routine ever since.
- **Hugo Toxxx - "Numero Uno"** — The 2005 diss aimed at Marpo, and the defining track of the mid-2000s gangsta wave. It made public beef, aggressive delivery and personal attacks a permanent part of Czech rap.
- **Rytmus - "Zlatokopky"** — From *Král* (2009), a Slovak track about gold-diggers. Song databases list it at 159 BPM in F minor, a double-time reading of a groove felt around 80 BPM. It is one of Rytmus's best-known songs and a common reference point for the shared CZ/SK scene.
- **Yzomandias feat. Nik Tendo - "Kawasaki"** — The 2016 single that set the Milion+ trap template. It is listed at 145 BPM (felt at 73 in half-time) in F♯ major, with a title taken from a motorcycle brand. It shows the money and vehicle flexing that defined Czech streaming-era trap.
- **Calin feat. Stein27 - "Růže"** — The 2019 Czech No. 1 that made melodic pop-trap the mainstream. It is listed at 80 BPM (160 double-time) in G major, with Auto-Tuned, half-sung vocals. It is the blueprint for romantic Czech pop-trap hooks.
- **Viktor Sheen - "Barvy"** — The 2020 track that shares its name with his album *Barvy*. It is listed at 90 BPM in C minor, with introspective, melodic rap in a minor key. It represents the reflective wing of Czech trap that later produced the Anděl-winning *Impostor syndrom* (2024).
- **Separ - "Flow Kmeť"** — The 2022 Slovak track that won the Ruka Hore song of the year. Its title puns on Separ's surname (Michael Kmeť). It showed the veteran DMS rapper still at the top of the CZ/SK scene a decade after his solo debut *Buldozér* (2012).
- **Calin & Viktor Sheen - "Safír"** — The 2023 No. 1 that the two melodic-trap leaders released in the same year as their joint album *Roadtrip*. It paired the two leading melodic voices of the 2020s and shows how far the mainstream had moved from its boom bap roots.
